## Problem
There exists disagreements among economists regarding the nature, causes, and solutions to business cycles. Two prominent theories are Real Business Cycle Theory (RBCT) and New Keynsian Theory (NKT). The first theory, largely associated with the Chicago school, views the cause of business cycles as exogenous shocks to the economy. One prominent shock is the introduction of a new technology. These shocks throw the market off its' current course and create busts which the market will eventually adapt to on its own. Consequently, their prescription is to have minimal government aid when depressions occur. In contrast, NKT combines parts of Keynsian Theory (KT), which views the cause of busts as decreases in consumer demand, with RBC. Reductions in consumer demand can be viewed as indogenous shocks, and they also make room for exogenous ones such as those included in RBC. Since they believe in indogenous causes, they believe in government incentives (such as increasing the quantity of fiduciary media) to stimulate consumer demand, driving investment and in turn production.

A less prominent but still potent theory is Austrian Business Cycle Theory (ABCT). Unlike the other two theories, their description of the business cycle starts at the boom. They claim increases in fiduciary media disproportionately decreases long-term interest rates compared to short and medium-term interest rates. Consequently, an over-insvestment in long-term projects occurs. Since the quantity of resources does not increase when the quantity of fiduciary media increases, the long-term investments cannot be completed. With the failed investments comes a recession where entrepreneurs figure out where resources are better allocated, then the economy is restored back to a stable state. It is obvious that from this point of view, the prescription is to not print money. 

## Why Ontology Might Help
Contrasting the Austrian approach with the rest, there is an ontological disagreement as to whether booms follow busts or busts follow booms. Certain causes only make sense if booms cause busts while others only make sense if busts cause booms. By getting on the same page about just this one ontological question, a great consensus can be had. 
Much of the difference between KT and RBCT/Austrian supporters came from their methodologies. RBCT/Austrian supporters had more faith in being able to build up from knowledge of micro-events than KT supporters. If it is in-fact correct that macro-economic phenomena reduce to micro-economic phenomena, then this lends credence toward the RBCT/Austrian camps. If that thesis is wrong, then KT and NKT theories hold more weight. 
These differences are not just semantic, but are rooted in deep differences about how reality is structured. Since the school's ontological commitments shape their methodologies, the differences are pre-empirical. Before having methodological debates, or arguments over how data should be structured/interpretted, laying every assumption of each individual study our explicitly would aid clarity. As well, it has been argued there is terminological slippage in Keynes' general theory by Henry Hazlitt. If this is the case, formalizing his theory into an ontology would undoubtably expose it. 

## Why Ontology Might Not Help
In the case of whether busts/booms are a cause/effect, it may be unncessary to use ontology. This question alone can be a background assumption of the economist which can be changed. While it may be worth-while to create ontologies for broader reasons, the amount of work needed to create an entire ontology to tie studies together over this classification would net the same results as justifying the cause/effect statuses at the beginning of research papers. 
Second, economics already has a mathematical structure to their models of the world. Upending their relatively pragmatic methodology would necessitate an overhauling of the field in general. One would have to prove that doing so would actually yield a better understanding of reality. It may be the case that time spent making ontologies perfectly map onto reality would better be spent conducting studies with simplifying assumptions --- especially since much of economics relies upon thought experiments which are inherently detached from reality.

### Rationale

In the case where one wishes to evaluate any given economic theory's explanation of the world, the first thing which they ought to evaluate is their ontology. For unempircal economics, this is clear. If one were to do economics as Mises did, all that can be evaluated is chains of informal logic. Formalizing his logic into an ontology would force every assumption to be laid out flatly, make it easy to understand, and its' validity clear. If it turns out to be valid, then his methodology and framework ought to be adopted. This would make consensus for the truth of Austrian Business Cycle Theory to be more probable than it otherwise would be.

Working backwards for empirical approaches to economics: It must be verified that all studies used to uphold a single view of the business cycle use compatible definitions which pertain to reality. If they are incompatible, then they measure different things and actually lend credence toward different theories. If they are compatible but do not map onto real entities, then that model does not describe reality --- no matter how good it is at making predictions. From here, one can either agree this is a problem or hail the pragmatic success of economics. If they choose to do the ladder, then they support economics as a means to predict what will happen rather than supporting economics as a means of understanding real causes.
