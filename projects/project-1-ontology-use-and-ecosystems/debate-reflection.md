## Issue Under Dispute

The issue under debate revolves around the following questions: 

1. Can ontology leave the umbrella of philosophy and join the sciences as a discipline? 
2. If it can, then what needs to be true for that to happen? 

## Reconstruction of positions and contrast

Since (1) only has two possible answers, I will address that question first. Denying that ontology can one day join philosophy for some comes from an unbridgeable chasm which they perceive between the two. Often, the differences are methodlogical. They may believe philosophy is largely thought to deal with intuitions, while science deals with hard empirical data and falsifiable propositions. To this crowd, scientific questions seem to have a more objective nature than philosophical questions.

Of course, not everybody believes that Ontology must remain unscientific. A quick glimpse at the emergence of science --- including physics, which is often thought to be the pinacle of science --- shows that many sciences originally came from philosophy. Through an optimistic induction, this may be enough to support the possibility of ontology emerging as a science. 

It is possible to grant this history but still deny the optimistic induction, however. After all, it seems more plausible that certain parts of philosophy are able to become sciences than others. Philosophers who are moral realists often think aesthetic judgments are subjective. It is obvious then that those philosophers would believe morality has more potential to be a science than aesthetics. An obvious question would be then, why should we believe that specifically ontology is capable of becoming a science? To this, one could point to the pragmatic falsifiability of ontological claims among many other things. If somebody makes an ontology of the economy but cannot represent certain economics events, then it probably needs work. 

Regarding (2), it is possible to give an infinite amount of answers. Instead of picking those answers at random, I will use some of John's answers instead. John emphasizes that in order for ontology to become a science, you need ontologists who are given standardized training like scientists. Laying out what this looks like, he goes on to describe certain methodological practices which ought to be taught to ontologists. One of these is an emphasis on modularity. A good ontologist, John argues, creates ontologists that are intended to 'communicate' with those which describe entities at a higher/lower level and adjacent areas. Through a process of numerous ontologists following these rules as a result of institutions who teach and enforce them, ontologies will be much easier to use and integrate with one another, building consensus and leading to development.

An obvious counter-position to John's first point would be to disagree with him on question (1). If ontologists are not scientists, then they should probably not be trained as scientists. Moving on further, the emphasis on modularity may lead to institutional incentives which cause ontologists to align themselves with those ontologies which are most popular rather than most true. With such a terrible incentive, some may view John's methodology as being pragmatically useful but detrimental to scientific success. 

## Personal Assessment

I view ontology as a science but have worries about John's philosophy on how to further ontology as a science. I do not have much original to add to the possibility of a philosophical discipline becoming scientific. My only input on this general topic would be that I have long viewed math and logic as a science, both of which are aprioristic in nature. They both seem to get at some sort of objective truth about classes of things. 

My main concern with John's philosophy revolves around the current state of the CCO and my affinity for Austrian economics. Within the CCO, there lies a definition for action and multiple things (such as goals) which can be put into relation with action. From an Austrian point of view however, there are numerous things which are excluded from this mid level ontology. As well, some of the terms in the CCO describe the same entity but have different names than all Austrian economists are used to. The fact that an Austrian ontologist's time would have to be sucked away to either fight with a mid level ontology's development team to make the changes they want, forgo using a mid level ontology completely, use a competing one with less networking advantages, or change those in their own school of thougt bothers me. These decisions do not seem to add to the scientific theory which I wish to develope as an ontologist, but detract from them. It seems to put me in a persuasive and pragmatic role rather than a truth-seeking one.

## Changing My Mind

I am generally skeptical of hierarchies in all aspects of life. Despite this, I love the idea of the BFO as a universal top-level ontology because of how broad and clean it is. Moving beyond this exception to my rule, in order to get me to adopt hierarchical modularity one would have to show me multiple ontologies intended to be one level lower than the BFO which also feel substantive and complete. I am only 60% sure this will not happen, but I am hopeful.
